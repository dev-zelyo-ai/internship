# Crypto & Web3

**Main Takeaway:**

Money solves the inefficiencies of barter by serving as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value. It must be divisible, fungible, portable, scarce, durable, and resistant to counterfeiting. Over time, money has evolved from physical commodities to fully digital forms, setting the stage for programmable and decentralized currencies.

---

## 1. Why We Need Money

Barter requires a **double coincidence of wants**, meaning each party must simultaneously desire exactly what the other offers. This makes direct exchanges slow and cumbersome, often requiring multi-step trades. Money eliminates this need by providing a universally accepted intermediary that everyone values.

---

## 2. Three Functions of Money

**Medium of Exchange:**

Money is universally accepted for purchasing goods and services, removing the need to find a specific barter partner. For instance, paying for groceries with cash or a credit card is instantaneous and frictionless.

**Unit of Account:**

Money provides a common measuring stick, enabling clear price comparisons and consistent accounting. When a laptop is priced at ₹50,000 and headphones at ₹2,000, budgeting and value assessment become straightforward.

**Store of Value:**

Money preserves purchasing power over time, allowing wealth to be saved and later spent. Keeping funds in a savings account or digital wallet ensures you can afford larger future purchases or pass assets to heirs.

---

## 3. Key Characteristics of Good Money

**Divisible:**

Money can be split into smaller units (e.g., cents, paise) to facilitate transactions of any size. This flexibility lets you buy a cup of tea or a car without awkward rounding.

**Fungible:**

Each unit is interchangeable; one ₹100 note holds the same value as any other ₹100 note. Fungibility prevents complications in exchanges and ensures fairness.

**Portable:**

Money is easy to carry and transfer, whether as lightweight banknotes, credit cards, or digital balances on smartphones. Portability underpins daily commerce from street vendors to e-commerce.

**Limited in Supply:**

Scarcity is enforced by monetary authorities or protocols to prevent runaway inflation. Central banks manage fiat issuance, while cryptocurrencies use algorithmic caps.

**Durable:**

Physical notes and coins are engineered to withstand years of handling, and digital records persist indefinitely. Durability protects the store-of-value function.

**Anti-Counterfeit:**

Security features like holograms, watermarks, and cryptographic signatures prevent unauthorized reproduction. This preserves trust and stability in the monetary system.

---

## 4. Evolution of Money

1. **Commodity Money:**
    
    Natural items with intrinsic worth—such as shells, cocoa beans, or livestock—served as early money. Their inherent value came from utility or cultural significance.
    
2. **Metal Money:**
    
    Metal coins (bronze, silver, gold) standardized weight and purity, enhancing portability and durability. Ancient China’s bronze “shell” coins and Greece’s gold staters exemplify this stage.
    
3. **Representative Money:**
    
    Early banknotes, like Song Dynasty jiaozi or gold certificates, represented claims on stored commodities. Holders could redeem paper for physical metals at fixed rates.
    
4. **Fiat Money:**
    
    Government decrees gave value to paper notes without commodity backing. Modern currencies—such as the US dollar and euro—rely on public trust and legal tender laws.
    
5. **Digital Payments:**
    
    Electronic records in bank ledgers and mobile wallets enable instant, borderless transfers. Debit cards, online banking, and mobile apps illustrate money’s fully digital evolution.

## 1. Three Functions of Money and Bitcoin’s Performance

## 1.3 Store of Value

Bitcoin has delivered dramatic long-term appreciation: 10,000 BTC used to buy two pizzas in 2010 (worth $41) grew to a market value of over $243 million by early 2023. While short-term volatility is high, the decades-long upward trend underlines its potential as a hedge against inflation.

## 2. Bitcoin’s Monetary Characteristics

## 2.1 Divisible

Each Bitcoin splits into 100 million satoshis, enabling micro-transactions—e.g., buying a cup of coffee for a few thousand satoshis without rounding issues.

## 2.2 Fungible

All satoshis are interchangeable, though coins linked to illicit transactions may be blacklisted by exchanges. In general, Bitcoin units remain uniform in value.

## 2.3 Portable

Bitcoin moves instantly via mobile wallets or hardware devices (e.g., USB-like hardware wallets), allowing global transfers without bulky coins or large cash holdings.

## 2.4 Limited Supply

## 2.5 Durable & Distributed

Over 14,000 full nodes worldwide store blockchain copies, preserving transaction history and network resilience. No single authority can erase or censor funds once confirmed.

## 2.6 Anti-Counterfeit & Secure

Strong cryptography and proof-of-work consensus prevent counterfeit coins. Ongoing development considers post-quantum cryptography to protect against future quantum attacks.

## 3. Why Bitcoin Matters

## 3.1 Inflation Hedge

With central banks massively expanding fiat money supplies (e.g., the U.S. Federal Reserve doubled its balance sheet between 2020 and 2022), Bitcoin’s fixed supply offers protection against currency devaluation and hyperinflation.

In crises—such as Ukraine’s cash withdrawal limits during conflict—Bitcoin holders maintain full access to funds. No government or bank can freeze, seize, or restrict on-chain balances.

## 3.3 Censorship Resistance

## 1. Defining Cryptocurrency vs. Digital Payment

Cryptocurrency is **digital currency** itself, secured by cryptographic protocols and stored in decentralized ledgers, whereas digital payments (e.g., PayPal, credit cards) are **methods** for transferring centrally issued money. In digital payments, intermediaries custody funds and can freeze or lose them if they collapse. In contrast, cryptocurrencies are bearer assets: ownership depends solely on private keys, eliminating custodial risk.

Centralized digital currencies include **Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs)**, issued and regulated by governments. Decentralized digital currencies—**cryptocurrencies** like Bitcoin and Ethereum—operate without a single authority, relying on distributed consensus across global nodes. This key difference affects censorship resistance, financial sovereignty, and risk profiles.

## 1. Ethereum’s Origin and Philosophy

Ethereum was created by Vitalik Buterin in 2015. Unlike Bitcoin's limited programmability aimed at maximizing security, Buterin envisioned a blockchain capable of running complex applications beyond simple payments. However, the Bitcoin community rejected major protocol changes, prompting Buterin to launch Ethereum as an independent platform focused on programmability without compromising decentralization.

## 2. Ethereum as a World Computer and DApps Platform

Ethereum functions as a **world computer** where every node in its global network runs the exact same decentralized applications (DApps) powered by smart contracts. These smart contracts are self-executing code stored on the blockchain that automate rules and transactions without intermediaries.

Applications on Ethereum include decentralized finance (DeFi), games, marketplaces, and identity systems, all running simultaneously on the same shared network infrastructure, ensuring censorship resistance and distributed trust.

---

## 3. Smart Contracts and Transactions

Smart contracts contain executable source code and represent the fundamental building blocks of DApps. Transactions on Ethereum are more than just cryptocurrency transfers; they represent **state transitions** updating the network’s global state, including account balances, contract data, and application logic.

---

## 4. Ethereum Account Types and Addresses

Ethereum supports two types of accounts:

- **Externally Owned Accounts (EOA):** Controlled by private keys belonging to users.
- **Contract Accounts:** Controlled by smart contract code deployed on the blockchain.

Both account types use hexadecimal addresses starting with "0x." A private key controls EOAs, while the code controls contract accounts. Users typically interact via EOAs, signing transactions to authorize fund movements or smart contract executions.

---

## 5. Gas and Transaction Fees

Gas is Ethereum’s mechanism to prevent infinite loops and denial-of-service attacks inherent in Turing-complete languages. It is a computational fee required to execute any operation:

- **Gas usage:** Quantity of computational steps consumed by a transaction.
- **Gas price:** Amount you're willing to pay per unit of gas, denominated in gwei (1 gwei = 10⁹ wei, smallest Ether unit; 1 Ether = 10¹⁸ wei).

Total transaction fee = Gas used × Gas price.

More complex operations like deploying smart contracts consume more gas than simple ETH transfers. Gas fees prioritize transactions in blocks during congestion: higher gas price transactions are processed first. This system resembles paying more for faster Bitcoin transactions during peak demand.

---

## 6. Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM)

The EVM is the virtualized computational environment running Ethereum smart contracts. It abstracts the underlying physical hardware so that smart contracts are executed uniformly across all nodes, regardless of their operating system (Windows, macOS, Linux).

This design ensures cross-platform compatibility and consistency, making Ethereum a global, decentralized "virtual computer."

---

## 7. Ethereum Improvement Proposals (EIPs)

EIPs are technical documents suggesting new features or protocol changes. For example:

- **ERC-20:** Defines a common standard for fungible tokens, enabling interoperability for tokens like USDC and Uniswap.
- **ERC-721:** Defines standards for non-fungible tokens (NFTs) representing unique digital assets like CryptoPunks or Bored Apes.

These standards enable broad innovation within the Ethereum ecosystem.

---

## 8. Difference Between Ether and Tokens

Ether (ETH) is Ethereum's native cryptocurrency, used to pay gas fees and stake in proof-of-stake consensus. ERC-20 and ERC-721 tokens are built atop Ethereum as programmable assets but require gas fees in ETH for transfers and smart contract interactions.

## 1. From High-Level Code to Bytecode

Smart contracts start as human-readable code (e.g., Solidity), which compilers transform into **bytecode**—hexadecimal representations of machine instructions executed by the Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM). Each bytecode instruction (one byte) corresponds to an opcode; for instance, **`0x52`** (hex) equals binary **`01010010`**, instructing the EVM to “store to memory.” This multi-stage compilation ensures portability across diverse hardware while maintaining efficient on-chain execution.

---

## 2. Core Components of a Solidity Contract

1. **Pragma and Imports:**
    
    Specify the Solidity compiler version (e.g., **`pragma solidity ^0.6.12;`**) and import audited libraries from OpenZeppelin (e.g., **`Ownable`** for access control and **`ERC20`** templates). This prevents reinventing well-tested code and enhances security.
    
2. **Contract Declaration:**
    
    Define the contract name (e.g., **`contract Bank is Ownable`**) and inherit ownership features via imported modifiers, enabling only the owner to execute privileged functions such as adjusting interest rates.
    
3. **Data Storage (State Variables):**
    
    Use **`struct DepositorInfo { uint256 balance; uint256 depositTimestamp; }`** and a **`mapping(address => DepositorInfo)`** to associate each user’s address with their balance and timestamp. State variables persist on-chain, forming the global contract state.
    
4. **Visibility Specifiers:**
    
    Functions and variables include **`public`**, **`private`**, or **`internal`** to control external access. For example, **`function deposit() public payable`** allows anyone to call the deposit function, while **`onlyOwner`** restricts rate changes to the contract owner.
    

---

## 3. Deposit, Withdraw, and Balance Functions

1. **Deposit Function:**
    
    Users send Ether with **`msg.value`**; the contract updates their **`balance`** and resets **`depositTimestamp`** to **`block.timestamp`**. This atomic update ensures accurate tracking of funds and time held.
    
2. **Withdraw Function:**
    
    Protected by **`require(balance > 0)`**, it calculates interest as **`principal × rate/100 × duration/1 year`**. Even one-second deposits accrue interest, lowering barriers to use. After transfer via **`msg.sender.transfer(amount)`**, it sets the user’s balance to zero.
    
3. **Balance Inquiry:**
    
    A **`view`** function (**`function myBalance() public view returns (uint256)`**) reads but does not modify state, enabling users to check their current deposit without consuming gas.
    

---

## 4. Access Control and Special Functions

1. **Owner-Only Actions:**
    
    The inherited **`Ownable`** contract provides an **`onlyOwner`** modifier. The **`setInterestRate(uint256 rate) external onlyOwner`** function allows the contract owner to adjust rates, safeguarding against unauthorized changes.
    
2. **Fallback/Receive Function:**
    
    The **`receive() external payable`** function enables the contract to accept raw Ether transfers. Without it, sending Ether directly to the contract would revert, preventing accidental fund loss.
    

---

## 5. Gas, the Halting Problem, and Fee Mechanism

Ethereum’s Turing-complete nature introduces the **halting problem**: some programs may never terminate. To prevent infinite loops and network congestion, every operation consumes **gas**. Transaction fees equal **`gasUsed × gasPrice`** (in gwei). More complex operations (smart contract deployment) require higher gas, while simple transfers cost less. During network congestion, miners prioritize transactions offering higher gas prices, akin to bidding for faster inclusion.

---

## 6. Security and Best Practices

- **Use Well-Audited Libraries:** Import OpenZeppelin contracts (e.g., **`Ownable`**, **`ERC20`**) to leverage community-vetted code and reduce vulnerabilities.
- **Validate Inputs:** Employ **`require()`** statements to enforce preconditions (e.g., nonzero balance) and revert invalid calls early.
- **Limit State Changes:** Minimize on-chain storage writes, as they are costliest in gas, and employ efficient data structures (e.g., mappings over arrays).
- **Explicit Fallbacks:** Define **`receive()`** or **`fallback()`** to handle unsolicited Ether and prevent fund loss.
- **Upgrade Patterns:** For production contracts, consider proxy patterns to enable safe upgrades without redeploying stateful logic.

## 7. Exploration Questions & Detailed Answers

**Q1: Why compile smart contract code into bytecode instead of running high-level code directly?**

Bytecode provides a uniform, low-level instruction set that the EVM understands, ensuring **cross-platform compatibility** and **deterministic execution** across all nodes, regardless of their underlying hardware or OS.

**Q2: How does the `onlyOwner` modifier enforce access control, and what are its limitations?**

**`onlyOwner`** checks **`msg.sender == owner`** before function execution, reverting unauthorized calls. Its limitation lies in centralizing control; if the owner’s key is compromised, attackers gain full privileges, so secure key management is critical.

**Q3: In what scenarios is the fallback `receive()` function necessary, and how does it differ from depositing via the `deposit()` function?**

**`receive()`** accepts plain Ether transfers without any function call, enabling the contract to collect funds sent directly. In contrast, **`deposit()`** updates internal accounting (balances and timestamps). Funds sent via **`receive()`** bypass accounting logic and may require separate retrieval methods by the owner.

**Q4: How does gas pricing influence smart contract design and user behavior?**

High gas costs for complex operations incentivize developers to **optimize code** and minimize storage writes. Users may batch transactions or choose off-peak times to reduce fees. Gas-efficient patterns (e.g., using **`uint256`** over smaller types) further lower costs.

**Q5: What upgrade strategies exist for long-lived smart contracts to fix bugs or add features?**

Proxy patterns, such as **Transparent Proxy** or **UUPS**, separate contract logic from storage. Users interact with a proxy that delegates calls to an implementation contract, enabling logic upgrades by updating the implementation address while preserving state.

# Comprehensive Report: How to Use Crypto

**Main Takeaway:**

Using cryptocurrency requires understanding self-custody versus custodial wallets, choosing between hot and cold storage, and securely managing private keys. Tools like MetaMask bridge users to Web3, while hardware wallets enhance security by isolating keys offline.

---

## 1. Wallet Types: Self-Custody vs. Custodial

Self-custody wallets (e.g., MetaMask, Ledger) give users full control of private keys, aligning with the principle of “being your own bank.” However, losing the key or recovery phrase means irreversible loss of funds. Custodial wallets (e.g., exchanges) hold keys on behalf of users, reducing key-management risk but exposing funds to third-party hacks or regulatory interventions.

---

## 2. Hot Wallets vs. Cold Wallets

Hot wallets remain online and are convenient for frequent transactions, but are vulnerable to malware, phishing, and network attacks. Cold wallets (hardware devices, paper or metal backups) keep keys offline, requiring physical access to steal, dramatically improving security at the cost of convenience and speed.

---

## 3. Key Generation and Recovery Phrase

Private keys are 256-bit random numbers; public keys and addresses derive mathematically. Wallets generate a **secret recovery phrase** (12–24 words) that deterministically recreates all derived keys and addresses. Storing this phrase securely—offline, in multiple locations—is critical: anyone with the phrase controls the funds.

---

## 4. MetaMask: Setup and Usage

1. **Installation:** Download the official MetaMask extension from metamask.io (look for millions of users and reviews) or from trusted browser stores.
2. **Account Creation:** Create a wallet password (only for local access) and secure your recovery phrase offline; confirming phrase order proves successful backup.
3. **Interface Overview:** Pinned to the browser toolbar, MetaMask displays account names, addresses (0x…), and balances. Users can switch testnets (e.g., Goerli) to practice without real funds.
4. **Transactions:** To send Ether or tokens, click “Send,” enter recipient address, amount, and confirm gas fees. Transaction hashes appear on Etherscan, showing status, block confirmations, value transferred, gas used, and burned fees post–EIP-1559.

---

## 5. Faucets and Testnets

Testnets (Goerli, Sepolia) simulate Ethereum mainnet for safe experimentation. Developers and new users obtain free test Ether via faucets (e.g., Goerli faucet on Alchemy), which require authentication to prevent abuse. Transactions on testnets mirror mainnet behavior, enabling learning of gas, confirmations, and block details without financial risk.

---

## 6. Hardware Wallet Integration

Hardware wallets (Ledger, Trezor) store private keys in isolated devices. MetaMask can connect to these via “Connect Hardware Wallet,” forwarding signed transactions without exposing keys. Purchasing only from official sources prevents tampered devices that could leak keys, ensuring maximal security for large holdings.

---

## 7. EIP-1559 and Fee Mechanics

Post-EIP-1559, each transaction burns a “base fee,” permanently reducing Ether supply, while users include a “priority fee” (tip) to speed processing. Burned fees can surpass staking rewards, making Ether deflationary over time—potentially increasing long-term value for holders.

---

## 8. Exploration Questions & Detailed Answers

**Q1: What are the trade-offs between self-custody and custodial wallets?**

Self-custody ensures complete control and censorship resistance but demands diligent key management and secure backups. Custodial wallets simplify user experience and recovery but introduce third-party risk and potential withdrawal limits.

**Q2: How does EIP-1559’s fee burn affect Ether’s economic model?**

By burning base fees each block, EIP-1559 reduces net issuance. If burned fees exceed staked rewards, Ether supply can shrink, creating deflationary pressure that may enhance long-term price stability and scarcity.

**Q3: Why is it important to use testnets before mainnet transactions?**

Testnets allow users to experiment with transfers, contract interactions, and gas management using valueless tokens, preventing costly mistakes on mainnet—ideal for developers and newcomers.

**Q4: How do hardware wallets prevent malware-based key extraction?**

Hardware wallets sign transactions internally; private keys never leave the secure element. Even if the connected computer is compromised, the attacker cannot access keys or generate valid signatures without physical access to the device.

**Q5: What best practices ensure safe storage of a recovery phrase?**

Write the phrase on fire- and water-resistant materials (metal backups), split backups across secure locations, avoid digital storage or photographs, and consider trusted custodians (e.g., family members in separate vaults).

# 1. DeFi vs CeFi vs TradFi — what’s different (with examples & tradeoffs)

**DeFi (Decentralized Finance)**

- **What:** Financial primitives implemented as smart contracts on public blockchains (Ethereum, BNB Chain, etc.). Examples: Uniswap (AMM DEX), Aave/Compound (lending), MakerDAO (DAI stablecoin).
- **How it behaves:** Permissionless (anyone can interact), composable (“money Legos” — protocols call each other), transparent (on-chain state visible).
- **Pros:** 24/7 access, censorship resistance, composability (integrations), transparent economic state.
- **Cons:** Smart-contract bugs, on-chain oracle manipulation, irreversible mistakes, complexity for end users.
- **Example scenario:** You supply ETH to a Uniswap pool and start to earn swap fees + token incentives, but also face price divergence on your assets.

**CeFi (Centralized Crypto Finance)**

- **What:** Centralized platforms that custody assets and provide exchange/lending services (Binance, Celsius (former), Nexo).
- **How it behaves:** Custodial accounts, off-chain order books in many cases, KYC/AML enforced.
- **Pros:** Familiar UX, off-chain matching (fast), customer support (sometimes).
- **Cons:** Counterparty risk (custodied funds), regulatory risk, potential withdrawal freezes.

**TradFi (Traditional Finance)**

- **What:** Banks, brokerages, regulated institutions; heavy compliance, settlement infrastructure.
- **How it behaves:** Centralized, regulated, slower settlement (except some RTGS/instant rails), strong consumer protections in many jurisdictions.
- **Pros:** Regulation, deposit insurance (in some countries), legal recourse.
- **Cons:** Often limited hours, intermediaries, higher friction for new innovations.

**When each is preferable (example):**

- Want instant, permissionless yield experimentation → **DeFi**.
- Want fiat on-ramp + easy UX and legal protections → **CeFi**.
- Need heavily regulated custody/insurance for large institutional flows → **TradFi**.

# 2. Stablecoin categories & mechanisms (detailed + example flows)

Stablecoins aim to keep value pegged to a reference (USD, EUR). Major categories:

### A. Fiat-Collateralized

- **Mechanism:** Issuer holds fiat reserves (bank deposits, short-term treasuries) 1:1 backing; issues tokens redeemable for fiat.
- **Examples:** USDC (Circle/Centre), Tether/USDT (partially audited history).
- **Pros:** Simplicity, low volatility if reserves truly exist.
- **Cons:** Centralization, custodial counterparty risk, regulatory exposure.
- **Typical flow:** User sends USD to custodial bank → issuer mints 1 USDC → user holds USDC on-chain → redemption burns USDC and returns USD.

### B. Crypto-Collateralized (over-collateralized)

- **Mechanism:** Smart contracts hold crypto collateral greater than the stablecoin supply (e.g., DAI). Peg maintained via collateralization ratios and liquidation.
- **Example:** MakerDAO/DAI.
- **Pros:** Trustless issuance (to some degree), transparent on-chain collateral.
- **Cons:** Requires overcollateralization, sensitive to crypto price volatility, complex liquidation mechanics.
- **Numeric example (DAI-style):**
    - Suppose collateralization requirement = **150%**.
    - To mint 100 DAI, user must lock collateral worth **150 USD** (e.g., 0.75 ETH at $200 each → 0.75×200 = $150).
    - If collateral value falls such that collateral ratio < liquidation threshold (say 125%), the collateral is liquidated.

### C. Algorithmic (mint/burn, seigniorage)

- **Mechanism:** Algorithms expand/contract stablecoin supply, or use redeem/mint mechanics across multiple tokens to maintain peg.
- **Examples:** FRAX (fractional approach), earlier Terra UST + LUNA (algorithmic, failed in 2022).
- **Pros:** Can be capital efficient if confidence holds.
- **Cons:** Fragile when confidence breaks — can enter a death spiral if redemption mechanics and demand fail (UST/LUNA example).

**Takeaway:** Choose stablecoin type by trust model: Fiat-backed for simplicity and low volatility (but centralized), crypto-backed for on-chain transparency (but requires overcollateralization), algorithmic for experimentation (higher systemic risk).

# 3. AMMs & constant-product formula (x·y = k) — how pricing, trades, and LP returns work

### Core idea

An AMM holds reserves of two tokens, X and Y, with reserves `x` and `y`. The product `k = x * y` is constant (ignoring fees). A trade moves reserves along the curve `x * y = k`, producing a price.

- **Spot price (approx)** for token X in terms of Y ≈ `y / x`. (That’s the instantaneous marginal price.)

### Example (step-by-step numeric swap)

Pool: `x = 1000` token X, `y = 1000` token Y. Swap **10 X** → get Y. Fee = 0.3% (Uniswap v2 style).

1. **Compute amount in after fee:**
    
    `amount_in_with_fee = 10 * (1 - 0.003) = 10 * 0.997 = 9.97`
    
2. **Compute amountOut (Uniswap formula):**
    
    `amount_out = amount_in_with_fee * reserve_out / (reserve_in + amount_in_with_fee)`
    
    Here `reserve_in = 1000` (X), `reserve_out = 1000` (Y).
    
    Compute numerator: `9.97 * 1000 = 9,970`
    
    Denominator: `1000 + 9.97 = 1009.97`
    
    Division: `9,970 / 1,009.97 ≈ 9.87158034397`
    
    So the trader receives **≈ 9.87158 Y**.
    
3. **New reserves:**
    - `x_new = 1000 + 10 = 1010`
    - `y_new = 1000 - 9.87158034397 ≈ 990.12841965603`
4. **Price impact:**
    - Price before = `y/x = 1000/1000 = 1` Y per X.
    - Price after = `y_new/x_new ≈ 990.12842 / 1010 ≈ 0.980325168` Y per X.
    - Price moved ≈ `0.980325168/1 - 1 = -1.9675%`. That is the price impact from swapping 10 X in this pool.

**Interpretation:** Large `amount_in` relative to reserve size increases slippage (price impact). Fees reduce the effective input and reward LPs.

### LP economics (qualitative)

- LPs deposit both tokens in ratio of current pool price and earn a pro-rata share of swap fees.
- If prices change significantly, LPs experience **impermanent loss** relative to HODLing both assets (we explain with numbers next).

# 4. Liquidity mining & yield farming — how incentives are structured (with a return example)

# 5. Impermanent loss — what it is, formula, and exact numeric example

**Definition:** If you provide a 50/50 LP position in an AMM and the relative price of the two tokens changes, the value of your LP position can be lower than simply HODLing both tokens outside the pool. It’s called *impermanent* because it disappears if prices return to original ratios; but if you withdraw while price is diverged, the loss is realized.

**Closed-form % IL for a price ratio change `P = newPrice/oldPrice`:**

`IL(P) = 1 - (2 * sqrt(P) / (1 + P))`

### Numeric example (step-by-step)

Start: deposit **1 ETH** + **100 DAI**. Initial ETH price = **$100**, so both sides are $100 each (50/50).

ETH price **doubles** → new price = **$200** → `P = 200/100 = 2`.

Compute LP vs HODL:

1. **k = x * y = 1 * 100 = 100.**
2. Solve for new ETH reserve `x_new` where `y_new = newPrice * x_new` and `x_new * y_new = k`:
    - `newPrice * x_new^2 = k` → `x_new = sqrt(k / newPrice) = sqrt(100 / 200) = sqrt(0.5) = 0.7071067811865476`
3. `y_new = newPrice * x_new = 200 * 0.7071067811865476 = 141.4213562373095`
4. **LP value in USD** = `x_new * newPrice + y_new = 0.7071067811865476*200 + 141.4213562373095 = 141.4213562373095 + 141.4213562373095 = 282.842712474619`
5. **HODL value in USD** = `1 ETH * 200 + 100 DAI = 200 + 100 = 300`
6. **Impermanent loss (absolute)** = `300 - 282.842712474619 = 17.157287525381`
    
    **Impermanent loss (percent)** = `17.157287525381 / 300 ≈ 0.05719096 = 5.719%`
    

**Interpretation:** If ETH doubles and you withdraw as LP, you’d be ~5.72% worse off than if you had just held the assets — unless fees and token incentives offset that difference.

**Mitigations:** provide liquidity in low-volatility pairs (stable/stable), use concentrated liquidity (Uniswap v3) to earn more fees per deposited capital, or hedge exposure using derivatives.

# 6. Price impact & slippage — definitions and example

**Price impact** = the instantaneous shift in price caused by executing a trade in an AMM (depends on trade size vs reserves).

**Slippage** = the difference between the expected execution price and the actual executed price; often includes front-running/mempool ordering effects.

**Example from AMM example above:** swapping 10 X in 1000/1000 pool changed price from `1` → `≈0.98033`, a **~1.97%** move. That is the *price impact*.

**User setting slippage tolerance:** If the UI slippage is 1%, the trade that causes price change >1% may fail (reverted by the router) — or execute and the user receives fewer tokens than expected.

**Practical advice:** For large trades, split into smaller trades, use larger pools (higher depth), or use limit orders via on-chain limit order protocols to reduce slippage exposure.

# 7. Front-running & sandwich attacks — how they work + numeric sandwich example

**Mechanics (high level):**

- When a user submits a transaction, it is broadcast to the mempool (pending tx pool) before being included in a block.
- **Front-running bots** scan the mempool for profitable transactions and submit their own transaction with a higher gas price so miners include it earlier (or miners/validators themselves can reorder).
- **Sandwich attack:** attacker places a *buy* before the victim tx (front-run) and a *sell* after the victim tx (back-run). The victim’s trade moves price unfavorably; attacker profits from capturing the price movement.

### Numeric sandwich example (step-by-step)

Pool reserves: **100 ETH** and **20,000 DAI** (so price ≈ 200 DAI/ETH). Fee = 0.3%.

- **Attacker** front-runs to buy **5 ETH**.
- **Victim (trader)** then buys **10 ETH**.
- **Attacker** back-runs and sells the **5 ETH**.

Computations (AMM math; rounding omitted for clarity):

1. **Attacker buy DAI required** to receive 5 ETH ≈ **1,055.798975875 DAI** (calculated via AMM inverse formula for amount_in given desired amount_out and fee).
2. After attacker trade, reserves ≈ **ETH 95**, **DAI 21,055.799**.
3. Victim buys 10 ETH; victim must supply ≈ **2,484.606640613 DAI** (higher than if attacker hadn’t front-run).
4. After victim trade, reserves ≈ **ETH 85**, **DAI 23,540.4056**.
5. Attacker sells their 5 ETH into the post-victim pool and receives ≈ **1,304.094260134 DAI**.
6. **Attacker profit** ≈ `1,304.094260134 - 1,055.798975875 ≈ 248.295284259 DAI`.

**Interpretation:** The attacker captured ≈ **248 DAI** by sandwiching the user’s transaction. The victim paid more DAI per ETH because of the attacker’s front-run.

**Mitigations:**

- Private transaction pools (e.g., MEV-aware relays like Flashbots) to avoid public mempool exposure.
- Batch auctions / frequent clearing to reduce incentive to reorder single txs.
- Slippage limits and usage of specialized DEXs that minimize front-run surface (e.g., concentrated liquidity with TWAPs, or protocols that execute off-chain matching).

# 8. Lending pools & on-chain credit — mechanics, examples, and risks

**Main primitives:**

- **Supply (lend):** users deposit assets and earn interest.
- **Borrow:** users borrow against collateral, paying interest.
- **Interest rates:** algorithmic, based on utilization (borrowed / total supply).
- **Liquidation:** if collateral falls below liquidation threshold, liquidators can repay part of borrow in exchange for discounted collateral.

### Example (numbers)

- Deposit **10 ETH** at price **$200** → collateral value = `10 * 200 = $2,000`.
- Suppose protocol **max LTV = 75%** → **max borrow = 2000 * 0.75 = $1,500**.
- Suppose **liquidation threshold = 80%** → if borrow value exceeds `2000 * 0.80 = $1,600` (or if collateral value drops causing borrow/collateral ratio to cross threshold), liquidation can occur.

**Leverage example:** Borrow $1,200 USDC against ETH, buy more ETH, repeat (recursive leverage) — amplifies profits and losses and increases liquidation risk.

**Interest rate dynamics:** many protocols use a utilization-based curve:

- `utilization = borrows / (cash + borrows)`
- interest rate = `base_rate + slope * utilization` (maybe a kink where slope increases after high utilization).
    
    **Example:** cash = 1000, borrows = 500 → utilization = `500/(1000+500) = 0.3333`. If `base_rate=2%`, `slope=10%` → rate ≈ `2% + 10% * 0.3333 ≈ 5.333%`.
    

**Risks:** liquidations, oracle manipulation (bad price feeds), undercollateralization if collateral rapidly drops in volatile markets.

# 9. DAOs (Decentralized Autonomous Organizations) — governance mechanics & tradeoffs

**Two principal voting patterns:**

- **On-chain voting (on-chain execution):** votes are tallied on-chain and smart contracts execute decisions automatically (transparent, gas cost). Example: MakerDAO governance for parameter changes.
- **Off-chain signaling (Snapshot) + on-chain execution by multisig/delegates:** gas-free voting signals, then selected delegates (or multisig) execute on-chain actions (lower barrier, introduces trust).

**Design considerations:**

- **Quorum & thresholds:** ensure decisions represent sufficient stakeholder size but not so high as to stall governance.
- **Delegation:** allows expertise to concentrate votes (liquid democracy), but increases centralization risk.
- **Treasury management:** DAOs manage protocol funds; multisig plus timelocks are common safety controls.

**Example flow (grant proposal):**

1. Community submits grant proposal (IPFS + Snapshot).
2. Snapshot vote signals support.
3. If quorum and thresholds pass, multisig executes spending after a timelock, or a governance executor contract executes automatically.

**Risks:** governance capture, low voter turnout, slow coordinate mechanisms for emergencies. Best practice: mix on-chain and off-chain signaling + multisig + timelocks + emergency pauses.

# 11. Exploration Q&A — answers with examples & context

**Q1 — How can LPs mitigate impermanent loss?**

- Use **stable/stable** pools (e.g., DAI/USDC) where price movement is minimal.
- Use **concentrated liquidity** (Uniswap v3) to concentrate capital in price ranges where trades occur — higher fee capture vs exposure.
- **Hedge** with derivatives or delta-neutral strategies (example: short the underlying to offset directional exposure).
- **Diversify** across pools and capture reward tokens that offset IL.

**Q2 — Upgrades to reduce front-running in DEXs?**

- **Private tx relays / MEV-aware channels** (Flashbots) to avoid public mempool.
- **Batch auctions** (periodic clearing instead of continuous-time), which reduce incentive to reorder.
- **Encrypted order relays** and commit-reveal schemes to hide trade intent until inclusion.

**Q3 — How did UST/LUNA show algorithmic risks?**

- UST used mint/burn with LUNA: when demand for UST fell and redemptions spiked, the mechanism kept minting LUNA to redeem UST, increasing LUNA supply, collapsing its price → **death spiral**. Lack of robust backing and low confidence made peg maintenance impossible under stress.

**Q4 — How do DAOs balance participation and efficiency?**

- Pair **off-chain signaling** (low cost, high participation) for preferences with **on-chain executive mechanisms** (gas cost, enforceability). Use delegation for informed voting and quorum thresholds to ensure legitimacy. Timelocks and multisigs provide checks and emergency controls.

**Q5 — How do lending pools adjust rates with utilization?**

- They define utilization `U = borrows / (cash + borrows)`. Low `U` → low APY, high `U` → APY ramps up (to attract lenders and discourage borrowing). Some designs use kinks: up to a threshold the slope is gentle, beyond it the slope is steep to protect solvency.

# Understanding NFTs and the Metaverse

## What Are NFTs?

NFT stands for Non-Fungible Token—a unique digital asset stored and tracked on a blockchain. The term "non-fungible" means each token is distinct and not interchangeable; for example, one bitcoin is equal to any other bitcoin (fungible), but each NFT is different and represents a unique item, be it digital art, a collectible, a piece of virtual land, or even a membership pass. The uniqueness of NFTs is enforced by encoding their ownership and metadata on a blockchain, typically using standards like ERC-721 or ERC-1155 on Ethereum. Each NFT is a digital unit that acts as a proof of ownership or authenticity for a corresponding asset, and since it’s recorded on a blockchain, the ownership and transaction history is public and transparent.

---

## NFT Use Cases and Examples

## Digital Collectibles

NFTs first gained notoriety with digital collectibles like CryptoKitties (unique digital cats on Ethereum) and CryptoPunks (pixel-art avatars). Collections such as the Bored Ape Yacht Club and NBA Top Shot moments highlight how NFTs can represent ownership of media, avatars, or moments in culture—with rare items fetching substantial value due to their perceived uniqueness and popularity.

## Digital Art

Digital artists use NFTs to certify ownership and originality of their artwork. The best-known example is Beeple’s "Everydays: The First 5000 Days," sold as an NFT for $70 million at auction. Unlike collectibles, NFT art often exists as one-off "1/1" pieces, extending provenance and royalties to the creator, sometimes automatically distributing a percentage (e.g., 10%) of each resale back to the original artist.

## Metaverse Land & Items

Virtual worlds like The Sandbox and Decentraland divide their landscapes into squares of land, each represented by an NFT. Owners can develop, sell, or monetize these parcels. Major brands and companies—both crypto-native and traditional—have bought land as digital real-estate investments or for marketing. Game items, avatars, and wearables in the metaverse are also issued as NFTs, providing players with verifiable ownership and the ability to resell or transfer assets outside of a specific game’s ecosystem.

## On-Chain Naming

Blockchains like Ethereum support naming services (e.g., Ethereum Name Service), issuing human-readable addresses as NFTs (e.g., alice.eth). This makes wallet addresses easier to remember, and some desirable names can themselves become scarce and valuable NFT assets.

## Memberships and Access

NFTs can act as keys to exclusive groups, services, or physical venues. For instance, clubs may issue NFT memberships for private dining or event access—ownership in the wallet is proof enough for entry, and memberships can be bought, sold, or transferred on open markets.

---

## How Are NFTs Made and Traded?

NFTs are minted (created) through smart contracts—typically using the ERC-721 standard—by executing "mint" functions that register a unique token ID to a blockchain address. Associated with each token is metadata, usually stored off-chain (like on IPFS filesystem), which includes links to the digital images or data that the NFT represents. Trading happens on NFT marketplaces (e.g., OpenSea), where users can list, buy, and sell NFTs, with blockchain transactions publicly recording each ownership transfer. While images and content can be copied, only the blockchain-registered owner holds the verifiable and transferable proof of ownership.

Creators often set royalties so that whenever an NFT is resold, a percentage of the sale automatically goes to the original creator. NFT collections with large communities and high trading volume can generate significant earnings for both creators and traders. The identity and uniqueness of each NFT are defined by its token ID and the contract address.

---

## NFT Marketplaces and Best Practices

The largest NFT marketplace, OpenSea, lets users search, buy, and sell NFTs from a wide variety of collections. Each NFT listing may include details like floor price (lowest available price in a collection), total trading volume, number of unique owners, rarity rankings, creator royalties, and more. Ownership transfers, minting events, and secondary sales are all visible on-chain and via block explorers like Etherscan.

Users must be vigilant against scams and counterfeit collections. Some creators use generic marketplace contracts (like OpenSea Shared Storefront), making it harder to verify provenance. The most reputable NFT drops usually deploy custom smart contracts and maintain verified profiles on marketplaces.

---

## Understanding NFT Storage

Most NFT images and files are not stored directly on the blockchain due to size and cost constraints; instead, their metadata—and links to where the underlying digital files can be accessed—are stored on-chain. These files are often hosted on decentralized filesystems like IPFS to prevent loss if a single host disappears. This ensures long-term access to NFT content, though it also means the NFT’s utility is somewhat reliant on these external networks.

---

## Myths and Issues: Rights, Cloning, and Copycats

Owning an NFT does not automatically grant intellectual property rights or prevent the digital file from being copied or shared online. Rather, the NFT represents a digital certificate of ownership and authenticity. Copycats and scams remain prevalent—especially given the permissionless nature of minting and trading on public blockchains—so buyers should always verify contracts and creators. Some NFTs explicitly extend commercial rights to owners (e.g., Bored Apes), but this varies by collection.

## What Is the Metaverse?

The metaverse refers to persistent and immersive 3D digital environments enabled by technologies like VR, game engines, and blockchains. In the crypto context, the metaverse encompasses virtual worlds such as Decentraland and The Sandbox, where users can interact, socialize, trade, and build experiences—owning land and items as NFTs. Unlike traditional web platforms, blockchain-based metaverse projects offer user-owned assets and, in many cases, open development for third-party creators.

The infrastructure for the metaverse is multi-layered. Some virtual worlds (e.g., Facebook’s Meta, Sims Online) run on centralized servers, giving companies full control over user data, access, and experience. Blockchain-powered metaverses leverage decentralized computation and storage—ensuring that even if one front-end goes offline, the data and ownership records persist on-chain, allowing new applications to access and build on the same assets and communities. This openness reduces the risk of platform lock-in and enables cross-platform creativity and longevity.

# Blockchain Scalability Issues and Solutions

## Understanding the Scalability Challenge

Scalability has become a central issue for major blockchains like Ethereum, especially as DeFi, NFT activity, and general adoption have exploded. The Ethereum mainnet currently accommodates around 15–30 transactions per second (TPS)—substantially lower than networks like PayPal (approx. 200 TPS) and Visa (potential peak: 24,000 TPS). This limited capacity has led to network congestion: during high-activity periods (such as the DeFi summer of 2020), both the number of daily transactions and the average gas (transaction) price surged dramatically. Users experienced slow processing times and prohibitively expensive fees, hindering broader usage and accessibility.

---

## Competing Layer 1 Blockchains

In response to Ethereum’s constraints, various alternative Layer 1 blockchains have emerged, often called “Ethereum killers,” boasting higher throughput and lower fees. These include EVM-compatible networks (BNB Chain, Avalanche, Polygon, Fantom) where developers can deploy the same smart contract code as on Ethereum, enabling seamless app migration and a familiar experience for users across wallets and explorers. Non-EVM-compatible Layer 1 chains (Cardano, Solana, Cosmos) feature their own architectures; for example, Solana leverages a novel Proof-of-History plus Proof-of-Stake consensus for much higher throughput (up to 870+ TPS in practice) and confirmation speeds under one second, trading off some decentralization for raw speed.

---

## The Ethereum Upgrade Roadmap

Ethereum is not standing still—its long-term upgrade roadmap (often called “Ethereum 2.0”) targets scalability and energy efficiency across several stages:

- **The Merge:** Transition from energy-intensive Proof of Work to Proof of Stake, completed in 2022, reducing energy use dramatically.
- **The Surge:** Introduction of sharding, splitting the network into parallel shards where validators process only subsets of transactions, enhancing throughput.
- **The Verge, Purge, Splurge:** Technical improvements to further compress data (e.g., moving from Merkle to Verkle trees), reducing storage needs for validators, and unlocking new scaling features.

These stages aim to make Ethereum performant enough for global usage without sacrificing its security or decentralization.

---

## Proof of Stake and Staking Protocols

Ethereum’s Proof of Stake model invites anyone to become a validator by locking up (staking) 32 ETH to process transactions and propose/validate new blocks. Validators earn annualized rewards (typically 3–5%) but risk slashing (partial loss of staked ETH) if they misbehave or go offline. For less technical or smaller holders, pooled staking solutions (e.g., Lido, Rocket Pool) let users participate with any amount of ETH by delegating to operator nodes, with risk and rewards pooled proportionally.

---

## Layer 2 Solutions: The Real Scalability Breakthrough

The most impactful advancements now come from Layer 2 (L2) scaling. These systems run on top of Ethereum, processing large volumes of transactions “off-chain” and only occasionally settling or posting proofs to the Layer 1 mainnet. The main L2 technologies are:

- **Optimistic Rollups (e.g., Arbitrum, Optimism):** Bundle transactions and submit them periodically to L1, assuming data is correct unless challenged (hence, “optimistic”). They allow easy code portability and robust compatibility with existing DApps, though withdrawals back to L1 may require waiting periods for fraud proof windows.
- **Zero-Knowledge Rollups (e.g., zkSync, Loopring, StarkNet):** Batch transactions and submit cryptographic proofs (validity proofs) that state changes are correct. These are faster to finalize (no challenge window) and can settle assets back to Layer 1 instantly. ZK rollups are computationally more complex but offer higher throughput and lower latency. Applications range from exchanges (Loopring) to general-purpose smart contract platforms (zkSync, StarkNet).

Other Layer 2 approaches include state channels (off-chain agreements), Plasma (child chains), and sidechains, but rollups are currently the most prevalent and supported by Ethereum’s core roadmap.

---

## Comparison: Layer 2 Rollups

| **Feature** | **Optimistic Rollups** | **ZK-Rollups** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compatibility | High (easy migration) | Lower, improving |
| Proof requirements | Simple, fraud proofs | Complex, validity proofs |
| Withdrawal Delay | Yes (wait period) | No (near-instant) |
| Ecosystem/Maturity | Robust, live | Rapidly growing |
| Finality | After challenge window | Instantly with proof |

---

## The Blockchain Trilemma

The “blockchain trilemma” asserts it’s hard to maximize scalability, security, and decentralization all at once. Layer 2 rollups—by shifting transaction processing “off-chain” but relying on Ethereum for security/finality—offer a credible solution, helping Ethereum scale to thousands of TPS without undermining trust or network health. Increasingly, major DApps (Uniswap, Synthetix, dYdX) deploy on these Layer 2s to improve user experience and cost.

# Key Pillars of Web3 and Metaverse: In-Depth Overview

---

## Fundamentals of Web3 in the Metaverse

## Cryptocurrency as Native Money

Cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin and Ethereum serve as the “native money” of Web3—used for payments, transactions, protocol governance, and incentive alignment. Much like the way physical currencies represent nations, major cryptocurrencies act as foundational currencies for digital economies by powering decentralized applications, NFT purchases, and in-metaverse transactions.

## Non-Fungible Tokens (NFTs)

NFTs are unique, non-interchangeable digital tokens built on blockchain technology. Their core value lies in representing ownership, provenance, and access rights for digital assets—ranging from art, virtual land, and gaming items, to memberships and audio/video content. The actual utility and privileges associated with an NFT depend on the rules encoded in the smart contract and the issuing project's roadmap. NFTs underpin exclusive access, digital identity, and new economic possibilities in Web3.

## Digital Wallets

A digital wallet gives users direct, self-sovereign control over digital assets (cryptocurrencies, NFTs, tokens, etc.) by leveraging private/public key cryptography. Unlike Web2's reliance on intermediaries, Web3 wallets offer decentralized management, empowering users to sign transactions, prove ownership, and engage with decentralized services while retaining full asset custody.

## Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs)

DAOs are community-driven organizations governed by smart contracts and transparent, on-chain voting. They enable wide participation in decision-making, automate proposal execution, and ensure governance aligns with the interests of token holders or community members. By reducing hierarchical bottlenecks, DAOs offer a new model for collaborative ventures with transparency and collective ownership, echoing the distributed ethos of Web3.

---

## Technologies Fueling the Metaverse

## Augmented Reality (AR), Virtual Reality (VR), and Mixed Reality (MR)

- **AR:** Integrates digital elements into the real world via devices like smartphones or AR glasses, overlaying information or interactive objects onto physical space.
- **VR:** Encompasses fully immersive digital environments, accessible through VR headsets and interactive interfaces, where users can socialize, build, and participate in virtual economies.
- **MR:** Merges AR and VR, blurring the boundaries between the physical and digital realms for deeper, context-aware interactions and applications.

## Gamification and Digital Avatars

Gamification leverages game-like elements—badges, leaderboards, missions, and incentives—to boost engagement, learning, and loyalty within virtual worlds and digital communities. Digital avatars enable users to represent themselves visually in virtual environments, participate in activities, and express identity—ranging from realistic 3D models to cartoon-style characters.

## Digital Assets and Co-creation

Digital assets now encompass not only cryptocurrencies and NFTs but also digital books, music files, licenses, in-game items, and intellectual property. Web3's collaborative environment fosters co-creation, where users, developers, artists, and brands can collectively design, build, and monetize new experiences or assets—breaking down the artist-audience divide.

---

## Practical Use Cases and Business Opportunities

- **NFT Utility:** Issuing NFTs that grant holders benefits like VIP event access, exclusive discounts, participation in raffles, or loyalty rewards—deepening customer engagement and enabling novel business models.
- **Virtual Venues & Training:** Building private metaverse venues or training centers for staff or customers—accessible only to NFT or token holders.
- **Hybrid Experiences:** Creating virtual pop-up stores, interactive advertising campaigns, or events that blend in-world metaverse activity with real-world participation, bridging digital and physical spaces.
- **Metaverse Design & Consulting:** Agencies like Small Circle assist businesses transitioning from Web2 to Web3, offering design consultation, virtual environment creation, NFT concept development, and integration support for SMEs confronting resource or skills gaps.

## Design Strategy for Metaverse Projects

Every successful metaverse project starts with strategic planning—mapping user journeys, defining aesthetics (from architectural style to lighting and theme), and anticipating user interactions. Platforms like The Sandbox stand out for accessible creator tools (Sandbox Game Maker, VoxEdit), allowing creators to build environments, assets, and avatars (using voxels) with relative ease. Detailed planning—from bird’s-eye layouts (“memory lane” concepts) to thematic storytelling—ensures engaging, scalable, and flexible virtual spaces.

- **Platform Selection:** Choosing a user-friendly, flexible platform is crucial for both creators and users. Sandbox’s accessibility and pixelated style (akin to Minecraft or Roblox) make it ideal for onboarding new designers and engaging broad audiences.
- **Customization:** Cultural elements can be seamlessly integrated into events or spaces (e.g., Chinese New Year themes, local landmarks) to make experiences unique and meaningful.
- **Creator Tools:** VoxEdit lets creators build custom assets (avatars, buildings, interactive props, etc.) that can be imported, traded, or minted as NFTs—enabling artists, agencies, and brands to commercialize digital art and products.

## Challenges and Ongoing Barriers

Despite substantial technological progress, there remain major adoption hurdles:

- **Lack of Standards:** No universally accepted definition or framework for an “open metaverse,” leading to fragmentation and interoperability challenges.
- **Web3 Knowledge Gap:** General awareness of blockchain, DAOs, NFTs, and metaverse concepts is still limited beyond crypto-native circles—hindering mainstream participation.
- **Market Skepticism (“Crypto Winter”):** Market downturns breed caution among users and investors, while hype cycles tend to attract short-term speculation rather than long-term builders.
- **Corporate Transition Challenges:** SMEs struggle to adapt due to limited resources, lack of in-house expertise, traditional workflows, or conflicting corporate cultures—necessitating external guidance, gradual integration, and skill-building support.
